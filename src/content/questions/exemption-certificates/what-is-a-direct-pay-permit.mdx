A direct pay permit is a state-issued authorization allowing a buyer to pay sales and use tax directly to the state rather than paying it to vendors at checkout. When a buyer presents one, you don't charge sales tax, and your liability for uncollected tax on that transaction is eliminated by the permit.

## How direct pay works

In a standard transaction, the seller collects sales tax from the buyer and remits it to the state. Direct pay reverses that: the buyer presents a permit to the seller, the seller sells without charging tax, and the buyer self-assesses what tax is owed and remits it directly to the state on their own return schedule.

The tax is still paid, it just moves through a different mechanism. For the seller, the effect is similar to receiving an exemption certificate: you document the permit, sell without charging tax, and the liability for that tax transfers to the buyer.

## Who gets direct pay permits

Direct pay permits are issued to large businesses, typically those that regularly make purchases where the correct taxability is difficult to determine at the point of sale. Classic use cases:

**Manufacturers** who buy inputs that may be used in both taxable and exempt production activities. Determining what percentage of an input is used in exempt manufacturing vs. taxable activity is easier done on an annual tax return than on a per-invoice basis at the time of purchase.

**Companies with complex mixed-use purchasing**, where equipment, supplies, or materials may be used for both taxable and non-taxable purposes and the allocation is only known after the fact.

**Large government contractors and enterprises** in some states that have negotiated direct pay status for administrative simplicity.

Most states impose a minimum purchase threshold (often $500,000 or more in annual taxable purchases) before issuing a direct pay permit. The application process runs through the state's Department of Revenue.

## What you do when a buyer presents a direct pay permit

**1. Obtain the permit number or a copy of the permit.** Most states issue a certificate or card with a permit number. Record this for your files.

**2. Verify the permit is active.** Many states have online lookup tools to confirm a direct pay permit number is currently valid. This step protects you, selling on an invalid permit doesn't transfer liability.

**3. Sell without charging sales tax.** Once you have a valid permit on file, apply tax-exempt treatment to qualifying transactions from that buyer for the applicable state.

**4. Retain the permit documentation.** Keep the permit number, a copy of the permit face (if provided), and a record of the transactions covered for at least 3 to 4 years: the standard audit-retention window.

**5. Apply the permit to the correct state only.** A direct pay permit issued by Texas covers Texas-sourced transactions only. The same buyer's purchases sourced to Ohio require Ohio documentation.

## What direct pay doesn't cover

A direct pay permit:
- Does not cover purchases in states other than the issuing state
- Does not cover all transaction types, some states restrict which purchases can be made under direct pay
- Does not eliminate the buyer's obligation to pay the tax (it just changes the payment mechanism)
- Is not transferable to other entities: the permit covers the specific legal entity that holds it

## The seller's liability after accepting a valid permit

A valid direct pay permit on file eliminates the seller's liability for uncollected tax on those transactions, the same way a valid exemption certificate does. If the permit turns out to be invalid, or if the buyer fails to remit to the state, the liability falls on the buyer, not on the seller who accepted the permit in good faith.

Protecting that good-faith status requires the same verification steps as exemption certificates: confirm the permit is active, retain the documentation, and apply it only within its stated scope.
