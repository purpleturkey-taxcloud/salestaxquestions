An exemption certificate is a document a buyer gives to a seller to claim that a purchase is not subject to sales tax. As the seller, collecting and retaining valid exemption certificates is how you protect yourself, without one, you may owe the tax even if you didn't collect it.

## What exemption certificates cover

Exemption certificates are used for **buyer-based or use-based exemptions**: situations where the same product would be taxable to one buyer but exempt to another, based on who they are or what they plan to do with it.

Common exemption types:

**Resale exemption:** The most common type. When a business buys goods to resell, they don't pay sales tax on the purchase, they'll collect it when they sell to the end customer. The buyer provides a resale certificate (sometimes called a reseller permit or resale certificate).

**Entity-based exemptions:** Nonprofits, government agencies, and some religious organizations may be exempt from sales tax on purchases for their exempt purpose. Each must provide documentation of their exempt status.

**Manufacturing and production exemptions:** Equipment or materials used directly in manufacturing may be exempt in many states. Buyers claiming this exemption provide an exemption certificate specifying the manufacturing use.

**Direct pay permits:** Some large businesses obtain direct pay permits from a state, allowing them to pay use tax directly rather than paying sales tax to vendors. These buyers will provide a direct pay permit in lieu of paying you the tax.

**Agricultural exemptions:** Farmers purchasing qualifying equipment or supplies may provide an agricultural exemption certificate.

## What certificates do NOT cover

Exemption certificates apply to buyer-based and use-based exemptions. They do not:

- Override product exemptions (groceries in an exempt state are automatically exempt, no certificate needed)
- Apply when the buyer hasn't provided one (you can't accept a verbal claim of exemption without documentation)
- Remain valid indefinitely without renewal (most states require periodic refresh)

## The seller's responsibility

When a buyer claims exemption, you must:

1. **Collect a complete, valid certificate** before or at the time of the exempt sale: the state-specific form or an accepted alternative like the SST uniform certificate
2. **Verify it's complete**: unsigned or incomplete certificates aren't valid
3. **Retain it**: for as long as the statute of limitations requires (typically 3–4 years after the period it covers)
4. **Apply it correctly**: the exemption applies to purchases within the scope of what the certificate covers

A valid, properly retained certificate shifts the liability to the buyer if the exemption turns out to be fraudulent or inapplicable. Without a certificate, the seller bears the liability.

## Product exemptions vs. certificate-based exemptions

Not every tax-free sale requires a certificate. When a product is exempt based on what it is, unprepared groceries in a state that exempts them, prescription drugs, exempt clothing: the exemption applies automatically. You don't need a certificate from the buyer for these.

Exemption certificates are only required when the exemption depends on who the buyer is or what they'll do with the product.

## Multi-state complexity

Each state has its own exemption certificate requirements. A resale certificate accepted in Texas isn't automatically valid in California. Managing exemption certificates across many states requires knowing which forms each state accepts, when they expire, and how to handle multi-state buyers.

The SST uniform exemption certificate simplifies this for SST member states, one form is accepted in all 24. For non-SST states, the Multistate Tax Commission's uniform certificate is accepted by some states but not all.
