Validate that a certificate is complete and plausible, you're not required to independently audit the buyer's exempt status, just to accept it in good faith.

## Key takeaways

- **Facial validity checklist:** confirm the certificate (1) uses the correct form for the state or a valid multi-state alternative, (2) has all required fields completed, (3) includes the buyer's name, address, and signature/attestation, (4) states a plausible exemption reason for the product being purchased, and (5) is not expired
- **Plausibility test:** a buyer claiming resale exemption for products they sell is plausible; a buyer claiming manufacturing exemption for office supplies is not: a mismatch between the claimed exemption and the product is a red flag
- **You are not required to verify the buyer's actual status:** you don't need to confirm the buyer is registered as a reseller in their state or verify their nonprofit status with the IRS; the buyer's attestation on the certificate is their responsibility
- **State registration number verification:** some states allow sellers to verify a buyer's sales tax registration number through the state DOR portal; this optional step adds a layer of confidence but isn't required for good-faith protection
- **Red flags that undermine good faith:** incomplete forms, obviously incorrect information (wrong state, mismatched business name), certificates for products that clearly don't fit the claimed exemption, or certificates from buyers with a known history of misuse
- **Retain what you accepted:** good-faith protection depends on being able to produce the certificate on audit; if you validated and accepted a certificate but didn't keep a copy, you lose the protection
- **SST good-faith standard:** SST member states have a defined good-faith standard; sellers accepting SST certificates through the SST program (including CSP-enrolled sellers) receive statutory protection
