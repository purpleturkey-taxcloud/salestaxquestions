Dietary supplements occupy an awkward middle ground in sales tax: they're neither food (in the exemption sense) nor prescription drugs. In most states, that means taxable at the full rate. For ecommerce supplement brands, state-by-state taxability mapping is more important than in almost any other product category.

## Why supplements are typically taxable

Food exemptions in most states are defined narrowly, items sold primarily for human nutrition and home consumption. Supplements and vitamins are generally excluded from these definitions because:
- They're not consumed as a primary food source
- They're regulated under DSHEA as dietary supplements, not as food
- Most state food exemption statutes predate the modern supplement industry and don't explicitly include them

Prescription drug exemptions don't apply either, supplements are sold over the counter and are not FDA-approved drugs.

## State breakdown by approach

**Taxable (full rate) — most states:** Including TX, NY, FL, PA, OH, NC, GA, WA, TN, and most others. Supplements and vitamins are taxed at the standard rate.

**Exempt or partially exempt:** A smaller set of states treat supplements more favorably:
- **California:** has historically exempted vitamins and dietary supplements meeting specific criteria
- **Illinois:** applies the reduced 1% rate (not full 6.25%) to vitamins and dietary supplements alongside other drugs
- **Some SST states:** the SST definition of "dietary supplement" creates a defined category that member states may treat differently, check each SST state's treatment

**State-specific edge cases:**
- Products marketed as "food" (protein bars, meal replacement shakes) may be treated as food rather than supplements in some states
- Products with an FDA Nutrition Facts label vs. Supplement Facts label may be taxed differently in states that use label-based tests
- Medical foods and products for specific medical conditions may be treated as exempt medical products in some states

## Product type variations

| Product | Typical treatment |
|---|---|
| Multivitamins | Taxable in most states |
| Protein powder | Taxable in most states; some states treat as food |
| Herbal supplements (capsules) | Taxable in most states |
| Protein/energy bars | Often treated as food if Nutrition Facts label; supplement if Supplement Facts |
| Meal replacement shakes | Varies significantly, often taxable |
| Prenatal vitamins | Taxable in most states; OTC, not prescription |
| CBD products | Highly variable and evolving by state |

## What supplement sellers should do

1. Build a state-by-state taxability matrix for your SKU types before configuring your tax engine
2. Use product tax codes from your tax software that match your specific product category, generic "supplement" codes may not capture state-level nuance
3. Monitor state guidance changes, supplement taxability is an active area of state revenue agency guidance
4. Don't assume food exemptions apply to your product without confirming the state's specific supplement definition
